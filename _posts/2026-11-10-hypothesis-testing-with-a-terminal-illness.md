My dad was diagnosed with glioblastoma in April 2026, and he died later that year. I spent those months as his advocate and medical liaison, and I approached the job the way I approach everything, as a scientist. I read the papers, learned the vocabulary, and wrote to clinical trial coordinators. I believed that if I understood the evidence well enough, we could make good decisions.

After his surgery, we were urged to do a six-week course of radiation, and we were promised he would bounce back afterward. He did not. He declined quickly, and we spent the rest of the summer not knowing whether the tumors had shrunk or grown. On an MRI, a growing tumor and a brain reacting to radiation can look alike, so each scan handed us a new question where we had hoped for an answer.

I think about it now as an experiment. The hypothesis was that radiation would shrink the cancer. But the treatment also added a variable. Once he was irradiated, every change in him had two possible causes, the disease and the treatment, and nobody could tease them apart. I would never accept that design in my own work. There was one subject, no control, and an intervention that confounded the only outcome we cared about.

I believe his decline was faster because of the radiation. We gave up good days for a little more time, and we made that trade because the hypothesis had to be tested. It was never presented to us as an experiment. It was presented as the standard of care.

The evidence behind that standard is real. It comes from trials with hundreds of patients, and it says that on average, radiation extends life. But an average answers a question about a population. My family was asking about one man and one summer, and science had no instrument for that. Science failed us. The method worked as designed, and it was not designed for us.
