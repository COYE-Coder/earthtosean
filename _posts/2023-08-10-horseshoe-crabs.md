In the summer of 2015, I was an REU (Research Experience for Undergraduates) student at the Dauphin Island Sea Lab in Alabama. I worked on the population ecology of the American horseshoe crab (*Limulus polyphemus*) in the northcentral Gulf of Mexico. The [paper](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0243478) came out in PLOS ONE in 2021, six years later.

We wanted to know which environmental factors drive horseshoe crab occurrence in this part of the Gulf. We also had a more practical question: can molts and carcasses stand in as indirect indicators of a horseshoe crab population? Direct population surveys are expensive and logistically difficult. If what washes up on shore carries a reliable signal, monitoring becomes much cheaper.

This was my first real research experience. I surveyed beaches, collected and cataloged specimens, and recorded environmental conditions. I also helped develop the research questions and the study design, and I contributed to the analysis of which environmental variables predicted horseshoe crab presence. I presented the work as a poster at the [AGU Ocean Sciences Meeting in 2016](https://agu.confex.com/agu/os16/meetingapp.cgi/Paper/88401) in New Orleans.
